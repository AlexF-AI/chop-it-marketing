# What to Cook With a Veg Box (Without Wasting Half of It)

*By [Alex Fahey](/author/alex-fahey), founder of Chop it. Published 3 August 2026.*

**Short answer:** unpack the box, identify the vegetables, cook leafy greens and anything bruised first, leave roots and squash until later, and plan four or five meals that share the contents. Chop it can shorten the awkward bit: photograph the open veg box, confirm what the scan found, add it to your pantry and find or create recipes around those ingredients. The final shopping list then contains the extras you need, not another round of vegetables.

A veg box is brilliant at bringing seasonal food into the house. It is less good at explaining what Thursday's dinner should be. This is a practical system for closing that gap.

## Quick answers

- **What should I cook first from a veg box?** Leafy greens, herbs and anything soft or damaged. Roots, potatoes, onions and squash usually give you more time.
- **How many meals should I plan?** Four or five is usually enough. Leave room for leftovers, changed plans or a meal out.
- **How do I find recipes for a mixed veg box?** Search for combinations rather than one vegetable at a time, or add the whole box to a pantry tool and plan across it.
- **Can I photograph a veg box into Chop it?** Yes. Photograph the open box, then confirm and correct the detected ingredients before planning.
- **Will the scan know exact quantities?** Not reliably. Treat it as a fast first inventory, not a set of kitchen scales.
- **What if I receive an unfamiliar vegetable?** Identify it first, then begin with a simple roasting, sautéing or steaming method so you learn its texture and flavour.

## Why veg boxes are both lovely and awkward

Veg boxes reverse the normal order of meal planning. At a supermarket you choose a recipe, then buy its vegetables. A box arrives first and asks you to work backwards.

That surprise is part of the pleasure. [The Soil Association notes](https://www.soilassociation.org/what-we-do/championing-organic/what-is-organic-food/organic-on-a-budget/) that box schemes can introduce a wider range of in-season food, support local farms and reduce plastic use. You cook things you might never have picked from a supermarket shelf.

The difficulty is not usually a lack of recipes. Search for "courgette recipe" and you will get thousands. The hard part is finding a small set of dinners that uses the courgettes, kale, leeks, carrots and aubergine together, in the right order, without requiring five unrelated shops.

That is a whole-box planning problem.

## Step 1: make the contents visible

Take everything out when the box arrives. Check for bruising, wilting or damage and identify anything unfamiliar. Store each item properly rather than leaving the whole delivery in its packaging.

Then make a quick inventory. A note on the fridge works. In Chop it, open the box so the vegetables are visible, photograph it and confirm the suggested items before adding them to the pantry.

The confirmation is not optional busywork. A camera may confuse chard and beetroot leaves, miss vegetables underneath others or guess the wrong quantity. It cannot judge an item's use-by date and it has no reliable way to know whether an aubergine is already soft. Correcting the list takes less time than typing the box from scratch and keeps the plan honest.

## Step 2: sort by urgency, not alphabetically

Divide the box into three loose groups.

**Cook first:** salad leaves, spinach, kale, chard, fresh herbs, mushrooms and anything damaged or very ripe.

**Cook in the middle:** courgettes, aubergines, broccoli, cauliflower, peppers, tomatoes and green beans.

**Keep for later:** carrots, parsnips, beetroot, potatoes, onions, celeriac and hard squash, provided they are stored well and still in good condition.

This is a guide rather than a guarantee. Condition matters more than category. A bruised carrot may be more urgent than a crisp cabbage.

Riverford's [guide to cooking with a veg box](https://wickedleeks.riverford.co.uk/lifestyle/how-to-cook-with-a-veg-box/) makes the same practical point: eat leafy greens early, keep roots for later and plan roughly five days out of seven so the week still has slack.

## Step 3: plan across the box

Do not assign one recipe to each vegetable. Look for meal shapes that absorb several ingredients and share the extras you need to buy.

Useful veg-box formats include:

- a traybake with beans, halloumi, chicken or sausages;
- a curry or dal that accepts greens and root vegetables;
- pasta with sautéed vegetables and a simple sauce;
- soup built around roots, leeks or squash;
- a frittata for cooked vegetables and herbs;
- fried rice or noodles for smaller odds and ends;
- a grain bowl with roasted veg, leaves and one dressing.

Choose four or five dinners, not seven. Build the first around fragile items, let two meals share one or two ingredients, and keep a forgiving "use it up" meal near the end.

If you use Chop it, the confirmed pantry gives AI and recipe search a better starting point. Ask for a plan that uses several vegetables together and state the real constraints: number of people, available nights, cooking time, diets and staples already in the cupboard.

## A worked example

Imagine the box contains:

- kale;
- two leeks;
- three courgettes;
- two aubergines;
- a bag of carrots.

A sensible four-dinner plan could be:

### Night 1: kale, leek and white bean stew

Use the kale while it is crisp and one leek as the base. White beans make it dinner rather than a side. Serve with bread if you have it.

### Night 2: aubergine, courgette and chickpea traybake

Roast both vegetables with chickpeas, garlic and spices. Make enough for lunch or save a portion of roasted veg for the fourth meal.

### Night 3: carrot, leek and red lentil soup

Use the second leek and part of the carrots. Lentils add body and keep the extra shopping cheap. Roots can wait until this point without driving the order of the whole week.

### Night 4: roasted-veg frittata

Fold leftover aubergine and courgette into eggs with any remaining kale or herbs. This is the flexible meal that catches smaller quantities before the next box arrives.

The additional shop is short and reusable: white beans, chickpeas, red lentils, eggs, garlic and perhaps bread. You are buying supporting ingredients for the box rather than buying a second set of vegetables.

## Step 4: build one list for the gaps

Once the meals are chosen, combine the missing ingredients into one shopping list. Check that list against the cupboards before buying.

This is where a connected pantry and recipe library earns its place. Chop it can merge ingredients across the planned recipes, subtract what you already confirmed and leave the gaps. It also keeps the recipes, whether they came from AI, a cookbook, a website or social media, in the same place as the plan.

The goal is not to let software dictate the menu. It is to avoid buying tahini for one recipe, crème fraîche for another and a new bag of spinach while the box's kale is still waiting.

## Step 5: give the end of the week an escape route

Even a good plan changes. Decide in advance what happens to vegetables that remain.

- Roast tired roots and keep them for soup, salad or a frittata.
- Blanch and freeze greens that will not be used in time.
- Turn soft tomatoes, peppers or aubergines into a sauce.
- Freeze soup and cooked dishes in labelled portions.
- Move an urgent vegetable into tonight's meal rather than protecting the original schedule.

Use appropriate storage and food-safety guidance for the specific ingredient. Freezing is a useful pause button, but it does not rescue food that has already spoiled.

## Why this matters beyond one box

[WRAP reports](https://www.wrap.ngo/what-we-do/future-proof-food/reducing-food-waste) that around 70% of UK food waste comes from households and identifies fresh fruit and vegetables among the main offenders. Its [2022 household report](https://www.wrap.ngo/resources/report/household-food-and-drink-waste-uk-2022) found fresh produce was the largest edible category wasted by weight.

The answer is not to stop buying vegetables. It is to make them easier to see and easier to connect to meals. A veg box is a good test because the ingredients arrive before the decisions. Photographing the contents, confirming the pantry and planning across the whole delivery turns that surprise into a useful starting point.

## Frequently asked questions

**What are the best recipes for a veg box?**
Flexible formats beat a fixed list: traybakes, curries, soups, pasta, frittatas and grain bowls. They can use several vegetables and tolerate substitutions.

**How do I use a veg box when cooking for one or two?**
Choose the smallest suitable box, plan fewer dishes that share ingredients and cook portions that become lunch or freeze well. Do not force seven separate dinners out of one delivery.

**Can AI identify every vegetable from a photo?**
No. It can create a useful first inventory, but overlapping produce, unusual varieties and poor light cause mistakes. Always confirm the result.

**Can Chop it make recipes from my veg box?**
Yes. Once the confirmed ingredients are in the pantry, use them to find or create recipes and build a weekly plan. Check generated recipes, particularly allergens, cooking temperatures and unusual quantities.

**What should I do with an unusual vegetable?**
Identify it confidently, check how it should be prepared and begin simply. Roasting or steaming it on its own teaches you more than hiding it in a complicated dish.

## The bottom line

The problem with a veg box is rarely the vegetables. It is turning a mixed physical delivery into four or five meals before the fragile bits fade.

Make the box visible, sort it by urgency, plan across several vegetables, buy only the missing extras and leave one meal flexible. Chop it can compress that workflow from a photograph into a pantry, recipes, a plan and one list. You still choose what sounds good. The software makes sure the box has somewhere to go.
