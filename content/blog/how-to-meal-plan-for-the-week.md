# How to Meal Plan for the Week (A System That Sticks)

*By [Alex Fahey](/author/alex-fahey), founder of Chop it. Last updated 3 August 2026.*

**Short answer:** the meal-planning systems people abandon are the elaborate ones. Colour-coded spreadsheets, batch-cooking marathons, a month mapped out in advance. The one that sticks is small: pick a handful of dinners for the week, write the one shopping list those meals need, and shop to it once. That is the whole job. Leftovers, theme nights and prepping ahead are polish on top of those three steps.

I have watched a lot of people try to build this habit, myself included. Below is the method that survives a normal week, and how to keep it going past the first keen Sunday.

---

## Quick answers

- **How do I start meal planning?** Plan dinners only at first. Pick 4–5 for the week, list the ingredients, and shop once. Don't try to plan every meal on day one.
- **How many meals should I plan?** Most households need 4–5 dinners a week. Leave the rest for leftovers, a takeaway or a night out.
- **When should I do it?** Whenever you shop. Many people plan Saturday or Sunday before the weekly shop. Pick a fixed slot so it becomes routine.
- **What's the biggest mistake?** Over-planning. A rigid 7-day plan breaks the first time life intervenes, and then the whole habit goes.
- **Does it save money?** Yes. Planning before you shop is one of the most effective ways to cut the bill and the waste, because you buy what you will use.
- **Do I need an app?** No, a list works. An app removes the tedious parts: building the shopping list and merging duplicate ingredients across recipes.

---

## Why meal planning is worth the half hour

Two payoffs, both real.

The first is the mental load. You answer "what's for dinner?" badly most days, at the worst hour: 6pm, tired, staring into the fridge. Planning moves that decision into one calm half-hour a week, made once instead of seven times under pressure. Skipping the blank 6pm fridge is the payoff people underrate, and the one I underrated until I fixed it.

The second is money. [WRAP estimates](https://www.wrap.ngo/media-centre/press-releases/sunday-15-march-average-uk-household-four-will-have-already-wasted) that an average UK household of four throws away food worth about **£1,000 a year**. When more of what you buy maps to a specific meal, less of it is left to die in the salad drawer. Planning is a practical way to spend less on food without eating worse.

## The system: five steps, half an hour

### 1. Check what you already have

Before planning anything, look in the fridge, freezer and cupboards. What's about to go off? What's lurking that you forgot you bought? Build a meal or two around that first. That is free food you have already paid for, and it is what separates a plan that cuts waste from one that adds to the shop.

### 2. Pick 4–5 dinners, not 7

This is the step that makes or breaks the habit. You do not need a meal for every night. Plan four or five dinners and leave the rest loose for leftovers, a quick fridge-raid, a takeaway, or eating out. A plan with slack survives a normal week. A rigid seven-day plan breaks the first Tuesday something comes up, and once it breaks people drop the whole thing.

Mix it up so you are not facing five involved recipes: a couple of quick 20-minute dinners, one that makes leftovers for lunch, one old reliable you can cook half-asleep, and one new thing if you fancy it.

### 3. Write the single shopping list

Go through your chosen meals and write down everything they need, then merge the duplicates. If three recipes use onions, write one line. Consolidating is the fiddly bit people get wrong by hand, and getting it wrong is how you end up with three half-used bunches of coriander.

### 4. Shop once, to the list

Do one shop against the list rather than several top-up runs. Frequent small trips are where impulse buys and duplicate perishables creep in. Buying once, to a plan, is what keeps both the bill and the waste down. Check the list against your step-1 inventory so you're not re-buying what you've got.

### 5. Stay flexible during the week

Treat the plan as a default you can move. Swap Tuesday's meal to Thursday when plans change, and cook the quick one on the night you are shattered. You are removing the daily decision rather than signing a timetable, and a plan you bend is a plan you keep.

## How to keep it going

Most people manage one good week and then drift. A few things keep it alive:

- **Keep a shortlist of 10–15 meals you can cook on autopilot.** Planning gets fast when you're choosing from a known set rather than inventing from scratch every week. Rotate one or two new recipes in so it doesn't get stale.
- **Pick a fixed planning slot and attach it to something you already do.** The morning coffee before the weekend shop, say. Habits stick when they're pinned to an existing one.
- **Leave room for comfort food.** The plan that lasts has a Friday pizza in it. A week of "ideal" meals you do not fancy gets abandoned faster than a realistic one.
- **Let some weeks be loose.** Planning three dinners beats planning none, and beats giving up because seven was out of reach.

## Frequently asked questions

**How many meals should I plan for a week?**
For most households, 4–5 dinners. Planning all seven leaves no slack for leftovers, takeaways or changed plans, and an over-tight plan is the most common reason people quit.

**What's the best day to meal plan?**
Whenever you do your main shop, commonly Saturday or Sunday. The key is a fixed, repeatable slot rather than the specific day, so it becomes routine instead of a decision.

**How do I meal plan on a budget?**
Plan around what you already have, lean on cheap staples (pulses, frozen veg, eggs, wholegrains), let one or two meals share ingredients to avoid waste, and shop once to a list. The planning itself is the saving, because it stops the impulse buys and the spoilage that inflate the bill.

**How do I meal plan for one or two people?**
Cook recipes that scale down cleanly, or make extra for lunch the next day. Buying for one or two without waste is the hard part, so favour ingredients you will use across more than one meal.

**Is meal planning worth it?**
If you find dinner decisions draining or you waste food, yes. It trades a daily 6pm scramble for one calm half-hour a week and can help you use more of the food you buy. If you enjoy improvising each night and waste little, you can skip it.

**What if my week never goes to plan?**
Then plan loosely: three or four meals with no fixed nights. A bendable plan you keep beats a perfect one you abandon.

---

## The bottom line

Meal planning works when it stays small: a handful of dinners, one merged shopping list, one shop, and the freedom to swap nights around. The over-engineered systems are the ones that fail. Start with four or five meals this week, plan around what is already in the fridge, and shop once. That is enough to feel the difference at the till and at 6pm, and it is a habit you can keep.
